---
title: Comparing everyday prices
level: starter
summary: Compare the cost per unit and the actual checkout total. The biggest package or largest discount may cost more than you can spend or use.
---

When you compare prices, check what you get and what you will actually pay. **Unit price** is the cost for one ounce, item, or other unit. It helps compare different package sizes. The [FTC explains how shelf unit prices help shoppers compare products](https://consumer.ftc.gov/node/86527).

## Compare the same amount

Sam is buying rice for dinners at home. The shelf prices below are fictional. Divide the price by the number of ounces to find the price per ounce.

```table Rice unit prices
| Bag | Price | Amount | Price per ounce |
| Small | $3.00 | 16 ounces | About $0.19 |
| Large | $5.00 | 32 ounces | About $0.16 |
```

The larger bag costs less per ounce, but Sam has only $4 in cash and the household may not use 32 ounces before the rice gets stale. The small bag is affordable today and avoids likely waste. A lower unit price is useful only if the total fits your cash and you can use the amount.

## Check discounts, tax, and delivery

An advertised discount lowers the listed price, but checkout can add costs. In this practice example, use a **hypothetical 8% sales tax** on the item, with no tax on delivery for this exercise. Actual tax rules and rates vary by location and item. The online store charges a delivery fee.

```ledger Online snack order checkout practice
Listed price | $12.00
- 25% discount (12 × 0.25) | $3.00
= Discounted item price | $9.00
+ Hypothetical sales tax (9 × 0.08) | $0.72
+ Delivery fee | $4.00
= Checkout total | $13.72
```

The sale price is $9, but the full total is $13.72. The [FTC recommends checking the total cost, including shipping, delivery, taxes, and other fees](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping). Before ordering, compare that total with the price of buying locally and include the time or transportation cost if it matters.

## Match the purchase to your cash and needs

Sam's household needs 16 ounces this week and has $4 cash for rice. A 32-ounce bag may be cheaper per ounce, but it costs $5 today. If half is likely to go stale, the saved unit price may not be worth the waste. Sam can buy the smaller bag within the cash limit, or check whether the household can safely store and use the larger one before deciding.

```table What to compare before choosing
| Check | Question |
| Unit price | What does each ounce or item cost? |
| Discount | What is the price after the discount? |
| Checkout total | What will tax and delivery add? |
| Cash limit | Can I pay the total now without borrowing? |
| Amount and waste | Will I use it before it goes bad or is no longer useful? |
```

```takeaways
- Divide price by quantity to compare unit prices.
- Recalculate the discounted price, then include tax and fees in the checkout total.
- A lower unit price may not be a better choice if it exceeds your cash or goes unused.
- Compare the total cost with what you need and can use.
```
