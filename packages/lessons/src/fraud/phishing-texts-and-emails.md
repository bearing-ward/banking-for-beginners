---
title: Phishing texts and emails
level: starter
summary: Fake texts from "your bank," a delivery company, or a toll road try to get your password or a one-time code. Here is how to spot them and what to do instead.
---

**Phishing** is when a scammer pretends to be a company you trust so you will click a link, log in on a fake site, or hand over a code. The message often looks real: the right logo, a believable problem, a link that looks almost right.

Phishing works because the problems sound normal. Packages do get delayed. Tolls do go unpaid. Banks do lock cards. The scammer is betting you will fix the "problem" from the link instead of checking.

```message A toll you do not remember
Channel: text
From: +1 (602) 555-0137
STATE TOLL SERVICES: You have an unpaid toll balance of $6.85. Pay by today to avoid a $50 late fee and a hold on your license: tollpay-state-services.info
! A small amount so it feels easy to pay
! A late fee and a license threat as pressure
! A link that is not your state's official toll site
```

## The package that "could not be delivered"

Delivery texts are some of the most common phishing messages, because almost everyone is waiting on something. The link usually leads to a page that asks for a small "redelivery fee" and your card number. The fee is not the goal. Your card number is.

```message A delivery text
Channel: text
From: +1 (415) 555-0162
ParcelPoint: Your package is on hold due to an incomplete address. Update within 12 hours or it will be returned: parcelpoint-redelivery.top/track
! You did not give this company your number
! A short deadline
! A strange web address instead of the shipper's real site
```

## Never share a one-time code

A **one-time code** (sometimes called a verification code or 2FA code) is the short number a company texts you when you log in. It proves that you, the person holding the phone, are the one signing in.

Anyone who asks you to read that code back to them is trying to get into your account. That includes someone who says they are from your bank's fraud team, a gaming company, or a friend who "accidentally" sent their code to your number.

```message A call from "the fraud team"
Channel: call
From: Northfield Bank Fraud Dept, (312) 555-0118
"Hi, we stopped a suspicious $480 charge on your debit card. To cancel it, I'm sending a code to your phone. Read it back to me so I can verify you're the account holder."
! Asks you to read back a one-time code
! Caller ID can be faked to show any name
! Pressure about a scary charge
```

> [!warning] Your bank will never call, text, or email to ask for a one-time code, your PIN, or your password. If anyone asks, hang up.

## What to do instead

You do not need to decide whether a message is real. Go around it:

- Open the official app, or type the company's website yourself.
- Call the number on the back of your card or on your statement.
- For tolls, look up your state's official toll agency. For packages, use the tracking number from the store where you ordered.

If the problem is real, you will see it there. Then delete the text, or forward it to 7726 (SPAM) so your carrier can block the sender. You can also report phishing at [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov). The FTC explains more at [consumer.ftc.gov](https://consumer.ftc.gov).

```takeaways
- Phishing messages pretend to be a company you trust and send you to a fake link.
- Do not fix a problem from a link. Use the official app or a number you look up yourself.
- Never share a one-time code, PIN, or password with anyone, even "your bank."
- Caller ID and sender names can be faked.
```
