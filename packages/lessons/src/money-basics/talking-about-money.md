title: Talking about money
level: starter
summary: Money talk can feel awkward, but a few good questions to family and trusted adults can teach you more than any class.
---

In a lot of families, money is not something people talk about. That can leave you guessing about how much things cost, how bills get paid, and what you will need to handle on your own. The people around you already know a lot of this. Asking is one of the cheapest ways to learn.

## Why it is worth the awkwardness

The adults in your life have paid rent, opened accounts, filed taxes, and probably made a money mistake or two. Hearing how it went for them helps you avoid the same surprises. It also helps them see you are getting ready to handle things yourself.

You do not need to ask how much anyone earns. Questions about how things work are easier to answer and often more useful.

```table Good first questions
| Topic | Try asking |
| Bills | "Which bills come every month, and about how much is each one?" |
| Groceries | "What does a normal week of groceries cost for us?" |
| Banking | "How did you pick your bank? What would you do differently?" |
| Mistakes | "What is one money mistake you made at my age?" |
| Taxes | "Can I watch when you do your taxes this year?" |
```

## Pick a good moment

Money can be a stressful subject, especially if things are tight at home. A calm moment, like a car ride or while cooking, works better than right after a bill shows up. Start by saying why you are asking.

```message Opening the conversation
Channel: text
From: You
Hey, I'm trying to figure out what it would cost to live on my own someday. Could you show me what our phone and internet bills look like sometime this week? No rush.
```

If your family does not want to talk about their own money, that is okay. You can still ask how something works in general, or ask another trusted adult: a relative, a teacher, a coach, a school counselor, or a staff member at your bank or credit union.

## Talking about money with friends

Friends often have different budgets. Saying "That is not in my budget this month, but I am in for something cheaper" is a complete sentence. You do not owe anyone an explanation. Suggesting a free or low-cost plan is often a relief for others too.

## When it is about your own money

As you start earning, some conversations become practical:

- Who will pay for your phone, gas, or car insurance?
- Will you be expected to help with household costs?
- Does a parent need to be on your bank account? Many banks require an adult co-owner on accounts for people under 18, and the rules depend on the bank and your state.
- Will a parent claim you as a dependent on their taxes? That changes how you file your own return.

Getting clear answers early heads off arguments later. Write down what you agree on.

> [!tip] The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has conversation ideas and activities for families on its [Money as You Grow](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/money-as-you-grow/) page.

Pick one question from the table and ask it this week.

```takeaways
- Family and trusted adults are a free source of real-world money knowledge.
- Ask how things work, not how much people earn.
- Choose a calm moment and say why you are asking.
- Agree early, in writing, on who pays for what as you start earning.
```
