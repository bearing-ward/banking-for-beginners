---
title: Securing your devices
level: starter
summary: Protect the phone or laptop that holds your messages, schoolwork, and money accounts. Prepare for a lost device before it happens.
---

Nia uses her phone for bus tickets, her first paycheck account, and photos of school projects. Protecting the phone helps protect all three. Start with the settings already built into your device.

## Lock it and keep it updated

Use a strong **passcode**, the secret code that unlocks your device. Avoid a birthday or an easy sequence. Fingerprint or face unlock can make everyday use easier, but keep the backup passcode private. Set automatic locking after a short idle period and lock the screen when you put it down.

Hide sensitive notification previews on the lock screen so a message or sign-in code is not visible to someone holding a locked phone. On a shared laptop, use separate user accounts and lock your account when you leave.

An **update** can repair security weaknesses as well as add features. Turn on automatic updates for the operating system, browser, and apps. Finish required restarts. Use the device's settings or the app's own update feature, not a pop-up advertising a security fix. [CISA recommends prompt software updates](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/update-software).

```table Nia's setup check
| Setting | What Nia chooses | Why it helps |
| Screen lock | A private passcode and short auto-lock delay | Limits casual access if the phone is left behind |
| Notifications | Hide message content while locked | Keeps private messages off the lock screen |
| Updates | Automatic updates, with restarts completed | Installs available security repairs |
| Shared laptop | Separate account, locked when away | Keeps another user out of Nia's open session |
```

## Download less and check permissions

Install apps from the device's official store or the developer's verified website. Check who published the app. Avoid cracked games, unexpected attachments, and downloads that ask you to turn off security protections. A familiar logo or many downloads does not guarantee safety.

A **permission** lets an app use something such as your microphone, photos, or location. Give it only the access it needs for the task. Review permissions in device settings and remove apps you no longer use. The [FTC explains how to limit app access](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-websites-and-apps-collect-and-use-your-information), and [CISA recommends trusted downloads and limited permissions](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/Cybersecurity%2520Awareness%2520Month%25202021%2520-%2520Protecting%2520Your%2520Digital%2520Home%2520Tip%2520Sheet_0.pdf).

```table Does this access fit the task?
| Request | Nia's response | Reason |
| Video-call app asks for the microphone during a call | Allow for the call if she trusts the app | The microphone is needed for speech |
| Flashlight app asks for contacts | Deny and reconsider the app | A light does not need her address book |
| Photo editor asks for all photos | Choose selected photos if available | It only needs the pictures she wants to edit |
```

## Have a backup and a lost-phone plan

A **backup** is a separate copy you can restore if a device breaks or disappears. Enable a regular cloud backup or use an external drive kept safely apart from your device. Protect the backup account, and check that recent files are included. A file stored only on the phone is not a backup. The [FTC's phone guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-protect-your-phone-hackers) covers locking, updates, backups, and finding a lost phone.

Set up the manufacturer's built-in find-device feature now. Learn how to reach it through the manufacturer's official website from another device. Keep a way to recover the account that does not depend entirely on the missing phone.

```table If Nia cannot find her phone after practice
| Situation | Safe next action |
| It may be nearby | Use the official find-device service to locate it or play a sound |
| It is missing or stolen | Use the service to lock or mark it lost; contact the carrier through verified support |
| A map points to an unfamiliar place | Tell a trusted adult and report suspected theft to police; do not confront someone |
| Someone sends a link claiming they found it | Open the official service independently; do not share passcodes or sign-in codes |
| Recovery seems unlikely | Follow the manufacturer's remote-erase guidance and understand what it deletes |
```

If a missing device could expose money accounts, contact those providers through verified channels promptly. Review account access from another trusted device. Remote locking or erasing may depend on the device's settings and connection, so do not assume clicking a button has already protected everything.

Today, check your lock settings, install pending updates, and confirm the date of your latest backup. Ask a trusted adult or school technology staff for help with a managed device rather than bypassing its protections.

```takeaways
- Use a private passcode, automatic locking, and prompt software updates.
- Download from trusted sources and limit each app's permissions.
- Keep a separate backup and prepare official find-device access before losing a phone.
- If a device is stolen, protect accounts and seek help instead of confronting a suspected thief.
```
