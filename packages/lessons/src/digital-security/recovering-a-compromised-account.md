---
title: Recovering a compromised account
level: next-step
summary: Take back an account safely, close extra ways an attacker could get in, and check what else was affected.
---

Luis's friends receive messages he did not send. His email recovery address has changed too. A **compromised account** is one someone else has accessed without permission. Recovering it means checking more than the password.

## Start through the real provider

Use a trusted, updated device. If you suspect the usual device has **malware**, software that can steal information or control the device, use a different device for recovery. The [FTC recommends a different device when malware may have exposed accounts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/06/how-spot-captcha-scam). Get help removing the malware before using that device for sensitive sign-ins again.

Open the provider's official app or type its known website address yourself. Use its hacked-account or recovery process if you cannot sign in. Do not use a recovery link or support number from an unexpected message or advertisement. For a school account, contact school technology staff.

Start with email if it is affected, because other accounts often send password resets there. If money is being stolen, contact the bank or payment provider immediately as well; do not wait to finish every recovery step. The [FTC's recovery guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recover-your-hacked-email-or-social-media-account) links to major providers' official instructions.

```table Luis chooses where to begin
| What Luis sees | Next step |
| Email password no longer works | Use the email provider's official recovery process on a trusted device |
| Social account is affected but email still works | Check email security, then use the social provider's official recovery process |
| School email is affected | Contact school technology staff through a known channel |
| A payment he did not make | Contact the payment provider or bank immediately using verified contact details |
```

## Close the ways back in

Once you regain access, follow the provider's security checklist. Set a long, unique password that you do not use anywhere else. Change it on any other account where you reused the old one. A password manager can help store different passwords.

Use the provider's option to sign out other sessions or all devices. A **session** is an existing sign-in that may let someone keep using the account without entering a password again. Review recent activity and report access you do not recognize. The [FTC's hacked-account checklist](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/10/email-or-social-media-hacked-heres-what-do) also recommends checking recovery information and email forwarding.

```table Luis's account cleanup
| Check | What Luis does |
| Recovery email and phone | Removes unfamiliar entries and restores his own verified details |
| Signed-in sessions and trusted devices | Ends other sessions and removes devices he does not recognize |
| Email forwarding, filters, and rules | Removes changes he did not make, including hidden forwarding or deletion rules |
| Connected apps and account access | Revokes access for unknown or unwanted apps through account security settings |
| Sign-in methods | Removes unfamiliar passkeys, security keys, or authenticators using provider instructions |
| Multifactor authentication | Sets it up again with his own trusted method and replaces exposed backup codes |
```

**Multifactor authentication**, or MFA, adds another way to prove it is you when signing in. Restore it after checking the enrolled methods, and keep new recovery codes somewhere safe outside the affected account. Never approve a sign-in you did not start or give someone a sign-in code. [CISA explains MFA](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/turn-mfa).

Changing a password alone may leave other access in place. An app you authorized can sometimes keep access until you revoke its permission. The [FBI explains this risk](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA260901). If a setting cannot be removed or suspicious activity continues, contact the provider's real support team and keep following its recovery process.

## Check the damage and warn others

Save screenshots of unfamiliar activity, notices, and transactions. Record when you contacted support. Check accounts that use the affected email for password resets and review them for changes or purchases you did not make.

Warn contacts through a separate trusted channel so they do not send money or open links from the attacker. You can ask a trusted adult to help organize the steps without sharing your passwords. Do not post account numbers, identity documents, or recovery codes publicly while asking for help.

```message Luis warns his friends through a separate account
Channel: text
From: Luis
My social account was accessed without my permission. The messages asking for money were not from me. Do not send money or open those links. I am using the provider's recovery process. If you already paid, contact your bank or payment provider now.
```

If money or card details were involved, contact your bank, card issuer, or payment app through its official app or the number on your card. Report unauthorized activity and ask what steps to take. The [FTC's scam recovery steps](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed) explain whom to contact. Recovery or a refund is not guaranteed.

Do not pay an unsolicited person who promises to hack the account back, and do not give them remote access or verification codes. Use the provider's recovery process. The [FTC warns about recovery scammers asking for fees](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/refund-and-recovery-scams).

For stolen personal information, follow the separate identity-theft lesson and [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/). For suspicious sign-in messages, use the phishing lesson's verification steps. Keep checking account activity after recovery and act on new changes you did not make.

```takeaways
- Recover through the real provider using a trusted device; secure affected email because other accounts may depend on it.
- Change reused passwords, end other sessions, and check recovery details, forwarding, connected apps, and sign-in methods.
- Restore MFA with methods you control. A password change alone does not guarantee the attacker is out.
- Contact financial providers promptly about money loss and avoid unsolicited paid recovery offers.
```
