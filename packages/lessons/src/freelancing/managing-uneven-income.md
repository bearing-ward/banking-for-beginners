title: Managing uneven freelance income
level: next-step
summary: A paid invoice is not all spending money. Keep records, reserve money for obligations, and plan for slow weeks.
---

One month you have three clients; the next you have none. **Cash flow** is the timing of money entering and leaving your business. Planning it helps you avoid using tax or customer money for everyday spending.

## Plan with money that has arrived

Leo, 18, tutors after school. He has $80 in business cash and receives $300 from completed sessions. Another client owes $100 next week. These amounts are hypothetical. The unpaid invoice is not available cash, and it may arrive late.

```ledger Leo's cash available today
Beginning business cash | $80.00
+ Payments received | $300.00
= Cash now available | $380.00
~ Unpaid invoice, not added | $100.00
```

Keep a list of invoices and due dates alongside your cash balance. Do not count a payment twice when it moves from "owed" to "received." A deposit for unfinished work may carry refund or delivery obligations, so keep enough available to meet those terms.

## Separate business obligations from personal spending

Leo must pay $40 for materials already bought and $20 for a business subscription. After reviewing his projected taxes with qualified help, he chooses to reserve $60 from this payment. That reserve is an example, not a tax rate or a calculation of what he owes.

```ledger Leo's allocation of available cash
Cash now available | $380.00
- Materials bill | $40.00
- Subscription bill | $20.00
- Moved to tax reserve | $60.00
- Kept as business cash buffer | $100.00
= Available for an owner transfer | $160.00
```

For a sole proprietor, an **owner transfer**, also called a draw, moves money from the business to the owner. It is not a deductible business expense and does not determine taxable profit. Moving money to a tax savings account does not pay the IRS either. Track it so you know which dollars are reserved.

The [IRS self-employed tax center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employed-individuals-tax-center) explains reporting business profit. Its [estimated-tax guide](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes) explains when payments during the year may be required. Setting money aside is useful, but waiting until filing may cause penalties if required payments were missed. State taxes may apply too.

## Prepare a low-income month

Suppose no more customer payments arrive for two weeks. Leo's $100 business buffer can cover $60 of expected business bills, leaving $40. It does not also cover his personal food and phone costs. He needs a separate household plan based on conservative income.

```ledger A slow period with no new payments
Business buffer | $100.00
- Expected business bills | $60.00
= Buffer remaining | $40.00
```

Before making a personal transfer, update expected receipts and bills. If work stays slow, reduce optional business costs, follow up on unpaid invoices, and revise what you can take home. Do not use borrowing to hide an ongoing loss. The [SBA finance guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/manage-your-finances) covers bookkeeping and cash-flow planning.

Store invoices, receipts, payment fees, refunds, and tax records safely. Reconcile them with your account regularly. If you use a separate account, check its age, ownership, and business-use requirements; separate records are still necessary.

```takeaways
- An unpaid invoice is not available cash.
- Reserve money for business costs, taxes, customer obligations, and slow periods before personal spending.
- An owner draw is not a sole proprietor's deductible business expense.
- Check whether estimated tax payments are required during the year; a savings transfer is not a tax payment.
```
