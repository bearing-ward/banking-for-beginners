An **IRA** is an individual retirement account: one you open yourself, not through an employer. A **Roth IRA** is the kind where you put in money you have already paid tax on (often none, for a teen), and qualified withdrawals in retirement, including all the growth, are tax-free. For a teenager with decades ahead, that tax-free growth can be worth a lot.

## You need earned income

You can contribute to a Roth IRA only if you have **earned income**: pay from working. A W-2 job counts, like lifeguarding or working a register. So does self-employment, like babysitting, mowing lawns, or dog walking, as long as it is real work you can document. Allowance, gifts, and investment earnings do not count.

In 2026, you can put in the smaller of two amounts: your earned income for the year, or $7,500.

```table How much can each teen put in for 2026?
| | Earned in 2026 | IRA limit | Most they can contribute |
| Priya, lifeguard | $3,200 | $7,500 | $3,200 |
| Diego, babysitting and lawn care | $1,450 | $7,500 | $1,450 |
| Sam, no job, $500 in birthday gifts | $0 | $7,500 | $0 |
```

The money you contribute does not have to be the exact dollars you earned. A parent or grandparent can give you money to contribute, as long as the total stays under your earned income. Keep records of your work: pay stubs, a W-2, or a simple log of babysitting jobs and payments.

> [!note] If you earn more than $400 from self-employment in a year, you need to file a tax return and pay self-employment tax on it. Ask a parent or check [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov) if you are not sure.

## Custodial Roth IRAs for minors

In most states, someone under 18 cannot open an investment account alone. A **custodial Roth IRA** solves that: a parent or other adult opens it in your name and manages it until you reach adulthood under your state's law (often 18 or 21). The money belongs to you from day one. Many brokerages offer them; compare fees before choosing.

## What one summer can turn into

Priya earns $3,200 lifeguarding at 16. She spends most of it and puts $1,000 into her custodial Roth IRA, invested in a low-cost index fund. She never adds to it. Assume, for illustration only, that it earns 6% a year compounded monthly until she is 66. Real returns rise and fall; this is not a prediction.

```ledger Priya's $1,000 contribution at 16
~ Assumed return | 6% a year
~ Years | 50
Contribution | $1,000.00
+ Growth | $18,935.96
= Balance at 66, tax-free if rules are met | $19,935.96
```

## The rules for taking money out

- You can take out your **contributions** (the amounts you put in) at any time, without tax or penalty.
- **Earnings** are tax-free once you are 59½ and the account has been open at least five years. Taking earnings out earlier usually means tax and a 10% penalty, with some exceptions.
- You have until the tax-filing deadline, usually mid-April of the next year, to contribute for a given year.

The IRS explains Roth IRAs at [IRS.gov](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/roth-iras), and [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov) covers choosing investments inside one.

```takeaways
- A Roth IRA grows tax-free for retirement, and teens can have one.
- You need earned income; you can contribute up to that amount or $7,500 in 2026, whichever is less.
- A custodial Roth IRA lets a parent open and manage the account for a minor.
- Contributions can come out anytime; earnings are meant for retirement.
```
