Retirement may feel far away, but the accounts built for it are some of the best tools you will get at your first full-time job. They come with tax breaks the government offers to encourage saving, and often with free money from your employer.

## What a 401(k) is

A **401(k)** is a retirement account offered through an employer. You choose a percentage of each paycheck to put in, and it is invested, usually in funds you pick from a list. (At schools and nonprofits, the same idea is called a 403(b).)

There are usually two tax options:

- **Traditional**: your contribution comes out before income tax, so you pay less tax now. You pay income tax when you take the money out in retirement.
- **Roth**: your contribution comes out after tax. Your withdrawals in retirement, including all the growth, are tax-free if you follow the rules.

In 2026, you can contribute up to $24,500 of your own pay to a 401(k). Most people put in far less, and that is fine.

## The employer match

Many employers add money when you contribute. This is the **match**. A common formula: the employer adds 50 cents for every dollar you put in, up to 6% of your pay. If you put in less than that, you leave part of your pay on the table.

Jordan, 22, starts a job paying $40,000 a year. Here is a year at 6%, then a year at 3%.

```ledger Jordan contributes 6% of pay
~ Salary | $40,000 a year
~ Match | 50% of what Jordan puts in, up to 6% of pay
Jordan's contributions (6%) | $2,400.00
+ Employer match (50% of $2,400) | $1,200.00
= Added to the 401(k) this year | $3,600.00
```

```ledger Jordan contributes 3% of pay
Jordan's contributions (3%) | $1,200.00
+ Employer match (50% of $1,200) | $600.00
= Added to the 401(k) this year | $1,800.00
# Matching contributions Jordan missed: $600
```

That $600 is part of the pay package Jordan gave up. Before anything else, try to contribute at least enough to get the full match.

> [!note] Some matches take time to become yours. This is called **vesting**. If you leave before you are fully vested, you may lose some of the employer's money (never your own). Your plan's summary explains the schedule.

## Rules to know

Money in a 401(k) is meant for retirement. If you withdraw it before age 59½, you usually pay income tax on it plus a 10% penalty, with some exceptions. When you change jobs, the account stays yours: you can leave it, move it to the new employer's plan, or roll it into an IRA.

Not every job has a 401(k), and many part-time jobs do not offer one yet. If you have earned income without a plan, an **IRA** (individual retirement account) is the do-it-yourself version. In 2026, the IRA limit is $7,500. The next lesson covers the Roth IRA, which fits teens well. Learn more at [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov) and the [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/retirement).

```takeaways
- A 401(k) invests part of each paycheck for retirement, with a tax break.
- An employer match is extra pay; contribute at least enough to get all of it.
- In 2026, you can put up to $24,500 of your own pay into a 401(k).
- Early withdrawals usually mean taxes plus a 10% penalty.
```
