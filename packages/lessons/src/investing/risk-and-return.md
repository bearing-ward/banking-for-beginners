---
title: Risk and return
level: starter
summary: Investments that can grow more also tend to swing more. Here is what risk means, why time helps, and how spreading your money out lowers it.
---

**Return** is what an investment earns or loses, usually shown as a percentage over a year. **Risk** is the chance that the return is different from what you hoped, including the chance that you lose money. The two travel together: investments with the chance of higher returns usually come with bigger ups and downs.

Nobody pays you extra for taking no chances. A savings account pays a small, steady rate because your balance is safe. Stocks have historically averaged more over long periods because owners accept that some years they will lose money.

```table Risk and return, from calmer to bumpier
| | How much it can swing | Long-run growth potential |
| Insured savings account | Does not drop | Low |
| Bonds | Some | Moderate |
| Stock funds | A lot, year to year | Higher |
| A single company's stock | Can go to zero | Higher, or nothing |
```

## A drop and a recovery

Losses and gains are not symmetrical. If an investment falls 20%, it has to rise 25% to get back to where it started, because the gain is on a smaller amount.

Jalen, 18, put $1,000 into a stock fund. In a rough year it fell 20%. The next year it rose 25%.

```ledger Jalen's fund over two years
Starting value | $1,000.00
- Year 1: down 20% of $1,000 | $200.00
= Value after year 1 | $800.00
+ Year 2: up 25% of $800 | $200.00
= Value after year 2 | $1,000.00
```

Jalen did not lose anything in the end, because he did not sell at the bottom. If he had panicked and sold after year 1, the $200 loss would have been locked in. These numbers are an illustration; real markets do not follow a script, and a recovery can take longer than a year, or longer than you would like.

## Time and spreading out

Two things lower the risk you take. The first is **time**. Over one year, stock prices are hard to predict. Over 20 or 30 years, good and bad years have tended to even out, though past results do not guarantee future ones.

The second is **diversification**: owning many different investments so that one bad one does not sink you. If you own one company and it fails, you can lose everything. If you own 500 companies and one fails, you barely notice.

> [!tip] A useful test: if this investment dropped by a third next month, would you need to sell it to pay a bill? If yes, it is money that belongs in savings for now.

Want to see how investors think about risk? The SEC's [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov) has a plain-language guide and a risk quiz.

```takeaways
- Higher possible returns come with bigger swings, including losses.
- A 20% drop needs a 25% gain to recover, so selling in a panic locks in losses.
- Time and diversification lower risk; neither removes it.
- Only invest money you will not need for years.
```
