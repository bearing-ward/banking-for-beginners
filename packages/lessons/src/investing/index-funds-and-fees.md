An **index** is a list that tracks a part of the market, such as the S&P 500, which follows about 500 large U.S. companies. An **index fund** owns everything on that list, so it rises and falls with the market as a whole. Nobody is trying to pick winners, which keeps costs low.

An **actively managed fund** pays professionals to choose investments they think will beat the market. That work costs money, and long-term studies have found that most actively managed funds do not beat their index once fees are counted.

## The expense ratio

Every fund charges a yearly fee called the **expense ratio**, shown as a percentage of what you have invested. You never get a bill. The fee comes out of the fund quietly, which lowers your return.

```ledger One year of fees on $10,000
~ Low-cost index fund | 0.05% a year
~ Higher-cost fund | 1.00% a year
Fee at 1.00% | $100.00
- Fee at 0.05% | $5.00
= Extra cost of the higher-cost fund, year one | $95.00
```

## Small fees, big gap

$95 a year does not sound like much. The problem is that fees compound, like growth does. Every dollar paid in fees is a dollar that is not growing for you for the rest of your life.

Here is an illustration. Nia and Theo each invest $100 a month for 40 years. Assume, for this example only, that the investments earn 7% a year before fees. Real returns are not steady, and nobody can promise 7%. Nia's index fund charges 0.05%, so she keeps 6.95%. Theo's fund charges 1%, so he keeps 6%. Growth is compounded monthly, like the Try it calculator.

```ledger Nia: 0.05% fee (6.95% a year after fees)
~ Monthly deposit | $100 for 40 years
You put in | $48,000.00
+ Growth | $210,824.84
= Balance after 40 years | $258,824.84
```

```ledger Theo: 1% fee (6% a year after fees)
~ Monthly deposit | $100 for 40 years
You put in | $48,000.00
+ Growth | $151,149.07
= Balance after 40 years | $199,149.07
```

They saved the same $48,000 and took the same market risk. The only difference was the fee, and it cost Theo $59,675.77. Try it yourself: put 6.95 and then 6 into the calculator's yearly return.

## How to check a fee

The expense ratio is listed in every fund's **prospectus** (its official description) and on its fact sheet. FINRA has a free [Fund Analyzer](https://tools.finra.org/fund_analyzer/) that compares costs, and [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov) explains other fees to watch for, like sales charges ("loads") and account fees.

> [!tip] When two funds hold similar things, the one with the lower expense ratio starts with an advantage that grows every year.

```takeaways
- An index fund tracks a whole market index instead of trying to beat it, which keeps costs low.
- The expense ratio is a yearly fee taken out of your return; you never see a bill.
- A 1% fee instead of 0.05% can cost tens of thousands of dollars over decades.
- Look up the expense ratio before you buy any fund.
```
