Not all savings are the same. Money for a flat tire needs to be ready tomorrow. Money for a car you will buy in a year can be locked up for a while in exchange for a better rate. Money for college or retirement many years away can take more risk. The question to ask is: **when will I need this money?**

## Certificates of deposit

A **certificate of deposit (CD)** is a deposit you agree to leave at a bank for a set time, called the **term**, such as 6 months, 1 year, or 5 years. In return, the bank usually pays a **fixed rate** that does not change during the term. At credit unions, the same thing is often called a **share certificate**. CDs at insured banks and credit unions are covered by deposit insurance.

```ledger Grace's 12-month CD
~ Term | 12 months
~ Rate | 4.00% APY, fixed
Deposit | $1,000.00
+ Interest for 12 months | $40.00
= Balance when the CD matures | $1,040.00
```

When the term ends, the CD **matures**. You can take the money out or roll it into a new CD. Watch the date: many banks renew it automatically at whatever rate they offer then.

## The catch: early withdrawal penalties

If you take money out before the term ends, you usually pay an **early withdrawal penalty**, often a few months of interest. The penalty is set by each bank, so read it before you open the CD.

```ledger If Grace takes her money out at 6 months
~ Penalty in her CD terms | 3 months of interest
Deposit | $1,000.00
+ Interest earned in 6 months, about | $20.00
- Penalty, 3 months of interest | $10.00
= What she gets back | $1,010.00
# Interest figures here are rounded to keep the example simple.
```

That is why a CD is a bad home for your emergency fund. Use one for money you are sure you will not need until a known date.

## Matching money to the right place

```table Where to keep money, by when you need it
| When you need it | Good fit | Why |
| This month | Checking | Easy to spend and pay bills |
| Any time, for surprises | Savings or high-yield savings | Safe, insured, reachable in a day or two |
| On a known date, 6 months to a few years out | CD or high-yield savings | A fixed rate if you can leave it alone |
| 5 or more years away | Investments, such as a retirement account | Can grow more over time, but values go up and down |
```

A **money market account** is another option you may see: an insured deposit account that often pays more than regular savings and may come with a few checks or a card. Compare its APY and minimums like any savings account.

Investing is a different topic with real risk, since investments can lose value. It makes sense only for money you will not need for years. Learn the basics at [investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/certificates-deposit-cds), which also explains CDs.

```takeaways
- A CD pays a fixed rate for a set term, with a penalty if you withdraw early.
- Use CDs for money you will not need until a known date, not for emergencies.
- Match each pile of savings to when you need it: checking, savings, CDs, then investing for the long term.
```

```quiz
? Why is a CD a poor place for your emergency fund?
- CDs are not insured
+ Taking money out early usually costs a penalty
- CDs never pay interest
> Emergencies do not wait for the term to end, and an early withdrawal usually costs part of your interest.

? You are saving $2,000 for a car you will buy in exactly one year and will not touch it before then. Which is a good fit?
- Your checking account
- Stocks
+ A 12-month CD or a high-yield savings account
> Both are insured. The CD locks in a rate for the year; high-yield savings keeps it flexible.

? Your CD matures and you do nothing. What often happens?
+ The bank renews it for a new term at its current rate
- The money is lost
- It turns into a checking account with fees
> Many CDs renew automatically, so mark the maturity date and decide in advance.
```
