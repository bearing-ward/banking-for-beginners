Maya saves money from her grocery-store job. A year later, her balance is higher, but the same groceries cost more. Counting dollars tells only part of the story.

**Inflation** is a rise in the overall level of prices over time. **Buying power**, also called purchasing power, is how much your money can buy. When prices rise, each dollar buys less. One item's price increase alone does not tell you the overall inflation rate.

## The same basket, a different price

Every price and rate in this lesson is hypothetical, not a current inflation reading or a forecast. Maya compares the same quantities and quality of three grocery items one year apart.

```table Maya's hypothetical grocery basket
| Item | First year | One year later |
| Rice | $10.00 | $10.50 |
| Vegetables | $20.00 | $21.00 |
| Other groceries | $20.00 | $21.00 |
| Total | $50.00 | $52.50 |
```

The basket costs $2.50 more. Divide $2.50 by its original $50 price to get 0.05, or 5%. That is this basket's price increase, not a measurement for the whole country.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks a much broader basket through the Consumer Price Index. Its [purchasing power guide](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/factsheets/purchasing-power-constant-dollars.htm) explains how price changes affect what dollars can buy. Your own spending mix can differ from that average.

## More dollars can buy fewer groceries

Suppose Maya starts with $500, enough for ten of her original baskets. Her account earns 3% APY for one year, with no deposits, withdrawals, fees, or taxes in this example.

```ledger Maya's hypothetical account after one year
Starting savings | $500.00
+ Interest at 3% APY | $15.00
= Ending balance | $515.00
~ Cost of ten baskets one year later | $525.00
~ Shortfall for ten baskets | $10.00
```

Her **nominal balance**, the dollar amount shown in the account, rose by $15. Her buying power fell: $515 divided by $52.50 buys about 9.81 baskets instead of ten. To keep buying ten baskets at the new prices, she would need $525.

Interest helped. Without it, Maya would have only $500 toward that $525 cost. It did not fully keep up with this basket's price increase. Fees and any taxes on interest can reduce what she keeps, too.

## Use prices when planning a goal

Deposit insurance protects eligible bank deposits if an insured bank fails, subject to coverage rules. It does not guarantee what those dollars will buy. See the [FDIC's deposit insurance guide](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance).

Inflation does not mean emergency money should go into an investment that might fall in value when you need it. For a near-term goal, check the current price, compare insured accounts' APYs and fees, and update the savings target as prices change.

```takeaways
- Inflation reduces what each dollar can buy when prices rise overall.
- A higher account balance does not always mean higher buying power.
- Compare savings growth with the prices of the things you are saving for.
- Deposit insurance does not protect buying power. Recheck your goal's price.
```
