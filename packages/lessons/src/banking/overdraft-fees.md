Kayla had $43.10 in checking. She forgot that her $40 phone bill would come out that morning, and after work she bought a $6.25 smoothie. The smoothie went through, and the next day her bank charged her a $35 fee. Her $6.25 smoothie cost her $41.25.

## Overdraft and NSF

An **overdraft** happens when the bank pays for something even though your account does not have enough money. Your balance goes below zero, and the bank often charges an **overdraft fee**.

An **NSF fee** (non-sufficient funds) is different: the bank refuses the payment and sends it back unpaid, and may still charge you. The company you were paying may add its own returned-payment fee on top.

```ledger Kayla's overdraft
Balance in the morning | $43.10
- Phone bill on autopay | $40.00
= Balance after the bill | $3.10
- Smoothie on debit card | $6.25
- Overdraft fee | $35.00
= Balance the next day | -$38.15
# Kayla now owes the bank $38.15 and must deposit at least that to get back to $0.
# When she opened her account, she had said yes to overdraft coverage without knowing what it meant.
```

Fees vary a lot. Some banks charge $35 or more per overdraft, some charge less, and some charge nothing. Some charge again if the account stays negative for several days. Check your bank's fee schedule.

## You can opt out for card purchases

By federal rule, your bank cannot charge an overdraft fee on ATM withdrawals or everyday debit card purchases unless you **opted in** to overdraft coverage. If you stay opted out, a card purchase that is more than your balance is usually declined instead. That is awkward at the register, but it costs nothing.

Opting out does not cover everything. Checks and automatic bill payments can still overdraw your account, so you still need to watch your balance.

```table Opted in or opted out?
| | Opted in | Opted out |
| $6.25 card purchase with $5 in the account | Approved, then a fee | Declined, no fee |
| ATM withdrawal over your balance | Approved, then a fee | Declined, no fee |
| Autopay bill over your balance | Can still overdraw | Can still overdraw |
```

## How to avoid overdrafts

- **Keep a buffer.** Pretend your balance is $50 or $100 lower than it is. That cushion absorbs a surprise bill or a hold.
- **Turn on a low-balance alert** in your bank's app, for example at $50.
- **Link savings to checking** as overdraft protection. Many banks will move money from savings to cover a shortfall for a small fee or none, which is far cheaper than an overdraft fee.
- **Know your autopay dates.** Put them on your calendar.

If you do get hit with a fee, call your bank and ask politely to have it removed. Many banks will waive a first one.

Read more about overdraft choices at [consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/bank-accounts/know-your-overdraft-options/).

```takeaways
- An overdraft lets your balance go below zero, and the fee can cost more than the purchase.
- You can stay opted out of overdraft coverage for debit card and ATM transactions.
- A buffer, a low-balance alert, and linked savings prevent most overdrafts.
```
