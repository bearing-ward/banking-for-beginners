Leo needs a $1,000 laptop for school. He has $200 saved. A store offers to let him take it home today and pay over the next year. That offer is **credit**: using money you have not earned yet, with a promise to pay it back later.

Credit shows up everywhere: credit cards, car loans, student loans, phone plans that split a new phone into monthly payments, and buy now, pay later apps. Each one is a **loan**, an amount you borrow and repay over time.

## Interest is the price of borrowing

A lender does not hand over money for free. It charges **interest**, a fee for using its money, figured as a percentage of what you owe. The **APR** (annual percentage rate) is that cost stated as a yearly rate, so you can compare offers side by side. A lower APR means a cheaper loan.

Say Leo borrows $1,000 at a 12% APR and repays it in 12 equal monthly payments. Each payment covers that month's interest and chips away at what he owes.

```ledger Leo's laptop loan
~ APR | 12%
~ Monthly payment | $88.85 × 12 months
Amount borrowed | $1,000.00
+ Interest over the year | $66.20
= Total paid | $1,066.20
```

The laptop cost Leo $1,066.20, not $1,000. That extra $66.20 is the price of having it now instead of saving up for a few months.

## The same item, three ways

How much credit costs depends on the APR and how long you take to pay. A higher rate or a longer time means more interest.

```table Paying for a $1,000 laptop
| Way to pay | Interest | Total cost |
| Save up first, pay cash | $0.00 | $1,000.00 |
| Loan at 12% APR, 12 months | $66.20 | $1,066.20 |
| Credit card at 24% APR, $50 a month | $289.88 | $1,289.88 |
```

## Why credit still matters

Credit is a tool, not a trap by itself. Used well, it lets you handle big costs like a car or college and builds a record that makes future borrowing cheaper. Used carelessly, interest piles up and you end up paying for things long after they are gone.

Before you borrow, ask three questions: What is the APR? What is the total I will pay? Could I save for this instead? The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/credit-reports-and-scores/) has plain guides on credit.

```takeaways
- Credit is borrowed money you repay later, usually with interest.
- APR is the yearly cost of borrowing; a lower APR is cheaper.
- A higher rate or a longer loan means you pay more in total.
- Before borrowing, find the total cost and ask whether you could save instead.
```
