Alex, 18, gets a call about an old phone bill. The caller wants a debit card number today. Alex does not recognize the amount. A demand for payment is a reason to check records, not proof that the caller or debt is real.

An **original creditor** is the company you first owed. A **debt collector** generally collects for someone else or buys debts to collect. The federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act covers many collectors of personal debts, but [generally does not cover an original creditor collecting its own debt](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-laws-limit-what-debt-collectors-can-say-or-do-en-329/). State laws may offer more protection. The [CFPB explains the difference](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-an-original-creditor-and-what-is-the-difference-between-an-original-creditor-and-a-debt-collector-en-1387/).

## Verify before sharing or paying

Ask for the collector's name, company, mailing address, and **validation information**, which identifies the creditor, debt amount, and dispute process. Collectors generally must provide it at first contact or within five days. Do not give an unverified caller your bank details or Social Security number.

Find the original company's contact information on your own bill or its official website. Ask whether it sent the account to that collector. Compare the notice with your bills and payment receipts. A caller knowing your name or part of an account number does not prove legitimacy. Use the [CFPB's guide to checking a collector](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-tell-if-a-debt-collector-is-legitimate-or-a-scam-en-1699/).

```message An unsolicited collection call
Channel: call
From: Fictional caller claiming to be Cedar Collection Services
You owe $180 for a phone account. Give me your debit card number now or you will be arrested tonight.
! A demand to pay before you can verify the debt
! A threat of arrest over a consumer debt
```

The [FTC's debt collection guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/debt-collection-faqs) explains that collectors cannot use false arrest threats or harassment. Save the message and contact details. You can report suspected scams to the FTC and collection problems to the CFPB through the links in that guide.

## Check the notice and the deadline

Alex independently confirms the collection company with the phone provider, then receives a notice. This is an excerpt from a fictional notice, not a complete legal form.

```table What Alex checks in the notice
| Notice item | Fictional example | Check against |
| Collector | Cedar Collection Services, mailing address provided | Independently verified company details |
| Current creditor | Harbor Phone Company | The old phone account |
| Account | Ending 0142 | Alex's own statements |
| Amount | $180, including itemized payments and fees | Bills and payment receipts |
| Dispute deadline | The notice's stated end date for the 30-day period | Calendar and date notice arrived |
| How to dispute | Mailing address and response instructions | Verified collector information |
```

Alex's receipts show a payment that may be missing. A **dispute** tells the collector that you challenge all or part of the debt. Under the federal collection rule, sending a written dispute within 30 days after receiving validation information requires the collector to pause collection of the disputed amount until it sends verification. The notice gives an end date. Read it promptly and use its instructions. The [CFPB explains validation and disputes](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-information-does-a-debt-collector-have-to-give-me-about-the-debt-en-331/).

Keep the notice, copies of your response and receipts, call notes, and proof you sent the dispute. Certified mail with a return receipt can help document delivery. Keep your originals. A phone call alone does not trigger the same written-dispute protection. If more than 30 days have passed, still ask questions and seek help; do not assume every right has disappeared.

```message Alex's written response to the verified collector
Channel: email
From: Alex
Subject: Dispute of account ending 0142
I dispute the amount claimed for this account. My records show a payment that is not reflected in the notice. Please send verification of the debt and the payment history. I am keeping a copy of this request and copies of the payment receipt.
```

This example could be sent by mail. Use email only through a verified electronic dispute method the collector provides. A dispute asks for verification; it does not automatically erase a valid debt. After verification, compare the response with your records before discussing a payment arrangement. Get any agreement in writing before paying.

## Court papers need a separate response

A collection notice and a lawsuit have different deadlines. A dispute letter to a collector does not replace a court response. If you receive court papers, read them and respond by the court's deadline, even if you think the debt is wrong or already sent a dispute.

Ignoring a lawsuit can lead to a **default judgment**, a court decision against you because you did not respond. Contact a legal aid office or an attorney promptly to understand the response required in your state. The [CFPB's lawsuit guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-if-im-sued-by-a-debt-collector-or-creditor-en-334/) links to legal help. A trusted adult can help organize records and find assistance. These are general steps, not advice about how to defend a particular case.

```takeaways
- Verify the collector and debt through independent records before sharing sensitive information or paying.
- Read the validation notice promptly. A timely written dispute gives specific federal protections.
- Keep copies and delivery records; a dispute does not automatically cancel a debt.
- Court papers have their own deadline. Get legal help and respond even if you dispute the debt.
```

```quiz
? An unknown caller demands your debit card number for a phone debt. What should you do next?
- Pay a small amount to prove you are cooperating
+ Request validation information and check independently with the phone provider
- Give your Social Security number to see if the caller knows it
> Check both the collector and the debt before paying or sharing sensitive details. A confident caller can still be a scammer.

? A verified collector sends a notice that leaves out a payment you made. You are within its 30-day dispute period. Which action preserves the written-dispute protection?
+ Send a written dispute through a verified method, request verification, and keep copies and proof of sending
- Wait for the next phone call and complain verbally
- Delete the notice because you have a receipt
> A timely written dispute requires a covered collector to pause collection of the disputed amount until it sends verification. Save the records that support your dispute.

? Court papers arrive after you disputed a debt. What should you do?
- Wait for the collector's reply, even past the court deadline
- Assume your dispute letter counts as a court response
+ Read the court deadline, seek legal help promptly, and respond as the court requires
> A collection dispute and a court case are separate processes. Ignoring court papers can lead to a default judgment.
```
