When you apply for a credit card, a car loan, or even an apartment, the company wants to know one thing: if we let you owe us money, will you pay it back? A **credit score** is a three-digit number that tries to answer that, based on your history with credit.

The most common score, the **FICO score**, runs from 300 to 850. Higher is better. A strong score can get you lower interest rates, easier apartment approvals, and smaller deposits on things like utilities and phone plans.

## The five things that make up a FICO score

FICO publishes roughly how much each part of your history counts. Two parts, paying on time and how much you owe, make up almost two thirds of the score.

```table What goes into a FICO score
| Factor | Weight | What it means |
| Payment history | 35% | Do you pay on time? |
| Amounts owed | 30% | How much of your available credit are you using? |
| Length of credit history | 15% | How long have your accounts been open? |
| New credit | 10% | Have you applied for a lot of credit lately? |
| Credit mix | 10% | Do you handle different kinds, such as a card and a loan? |
```

**Payment history** is the biggest piece. One payment that is 30 or more days late can hurt your score for years. Setting up automatic payments of at least the minimum is the easiest protection there is.

## Using a small part of your limit

"Amounts owed" looks closely at your **credit utilization**: the share of your credit limits you are using. A $300 balance on a $1,500 limit is 20% utilization. Lower is better, and many experts suggest staying under 30%.

```ledger Sofia's two cards
~ Balance on her statements | $300 of $1,500
~ Credit utilization | 20%
Card A limit | $500.00
+ Card B limit | $1,000.00
= Total credit limit | $1,500.00
```

Utilization is measured from the balance on your statement, so paying your card off in full each month keeps it low and costs you no interest.

## What does not count

Your score does not include your income, your age, your race, where you live, or how much is in your bank account. Checking your own score does not lower it either. Applying for new credit can cause a small, temporary dip, which is why the "new credit" factor exists.

There are many versions of credit scores, including VantageScore, which also uses 300 to 850. Lenders choose which one to use, so the number you see in an app may differ a little from the one a lender sees. The habits that raise one raise all of them.

## Starting from zero

Most teens have no score at all, because they have never borrowed. That is normal. A score starts once you have an account that reports to the credit bureaus, and it grows as you pay on time month after month. The lesson on building credit as a teen covers how to start.

```takeaways
- A FICO score runs from 300 to 850; higher means lenders see you as lower risk.
- Paying on time (35%) and keeping balances low (30%) matter most.
- Keep the balance on your statement well under your limit, ideally under 30%.
- Checking your own score does not hurt it.
```
