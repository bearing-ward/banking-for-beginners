Most teens have no credit history, so they have no score. That is not bad credit; it is no credit. But when you turn 18 and try to rent an apartment, finance a car, or get a phone plan in your own name, "no history" can mean a bigger deposit, a cosigner, or a higher rate. Starting a little history early, carefully, makes those steps easier.

## The rules for under 21

You generally have to be 18 to sign a credit agreement on your own. A federal law called the **CARD Act** adds another step for anyone under 21: to get a card in your own name, you must show you have **independent income** (your own pay from a job, for example) that can cover the payments, or have a **cosigner** over 21 who agrees to pay if you do not. Many card companies no longer accept cosigners, so your own income is often the path.

## Four ways to start

```table Ways to start building credit
| Option | How it works | Watch out for |
| Authorized user | A parent adds you to their card; their history can show on your report | Their late payments can hurt you too |
| Secured card | You put down a deposit, often $200 to $500, and that becomes your limit | Fees; make sure it reports to all three bureaus |
| Student card | A regular card with a low limit, for students with income | Higher APRs; easy to overspend |
| Credit-builder loan | A bank or credit union holds a small loan in savings while you make payments | Fees and interest; payments must be on time |
```

Becoming an **authorized user** is often the easiest first step, and you can do it before 18 at many card companies. You get a card on a parent's account. You do not have to use it at all. Their on-time payments and long history can help your report, but only if they pay on time and keep the balance low. Talk about the rules first.

## A secured card in practice

A **secured card** works like a regular credit card, but you put down a cash deposit first. If you do not pay, the bank keeps the deposit, so the bank takes little risk and approves people with no history. After you pay on time for a while, many banks return the deposit and move you to a regular card.

The best way to use one is boring on purpose: put one small bill on it, set up autopay for the full balance, and leave it alone.

```ledger Jamal's secured card, one month
~ Deposit and credit limit | $200 / $200
~ Utilization | 6%
Music streaming | $11.99
= Statement balance | $11.99
- Autopay, full balance | $11.99
= Balance left to charge interest on | $0.00
```

Jamal pays no interest, uses a small part of his limit, and gets an on-time payment reported every month. After a year of that, he has a real credit history.

## Habits that build a score

- Pay every bill on time, every month. Payment history is the biggest part of your score.
- Keep your balance low compared with your limit, ideally under 30%.
- Do not apply for lots of cards at once.
- Keep your first card open, even if you use it lightly. Older accounts help.

Be careful with anyone who offers to "build your credit fast" for a fee. There are no shortcuts. The [CFPB has more on building credit](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/credit-reports-and-scores/).

```takeaways
- No credit history is common for teens; starting early makes life after 18 easier.
- Under 21, you need your own income or a cosigner over 21 to get a card.
- Authorized user, secured card, student card, and credit-builder loan are safe starts.
- Charge one small bill, pay it in full on autopay, and keep the account open.
```
