Leo spent the summer running a small lawn care business for neighbors. He was paid in cash and through a payment app, and nobody took any taxes out. Leo is a dependent and earned too little to owe income tax, but he still owes one tax: **self-employment tax**.

## What it is

When you work for an employer, you pay 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare, and your employer pays the same again. When you work for yourself, you are both the worker and the employer, so you pay both halves: 15.3% in total. That is self-employment tax. It builds your Social Security record the same way payroll taxes do.

## Step 1: find your net earnings

Self-employment tax is figured on **net earnings**: what customers paid you minus the costs of doing the work, called **business expenses**. For Leo, that means gas for the mower, trimmer line, and a new rake. Keep receipts; every real expense lowers the tax.

```ledger Leo's lawn business in 2026
Paid by customers | $2,400.00
- Gas, trimmer line, and a rake | $400.00
= Net earnings | $2,000.00
```

## Step 2: apply the two percentages

The tax is 15.3%, but it is charged on 92.35% of your net earnings, not all of it. That adjustment gives self-employed people a break similar to the employer's half that employees never see on their pay. The 15.3% splits into 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare.

```ledger Leo's self-employment tax
~ Net earnings | $2,000.00
~ Counted earnings, 92.35% of $2,000 | $1,847.00
Social Security, 12.4% of $1,847 | $229.03
+ Medicare, 2.9% of $1,847 | $53.56
= Self-employment tax | $282.59
```

Half of the self-employment tax is also subtracted from your income before income tax is figured, which helps people with larger businesses. For Leo, income tax is already $0.

## Do you have to file?

Once your net earnings from self-employment reach $400 in a year, you must file a federal tax return, even as a dependent who owes no income tax. You report the business on Schedule C and figure the tax on Schedule SE, both attached to Form 1040. Free filing software and VITA volunteers handle both.

```table Leo's filing checklist
| Form | What it does | Leo's number |
| Schedule C | Business income and expenses | $2,000.00 net |
| Schedule SE | Figures self-employment tax | $282.59 |
| Form 1040 | The main return | Owes $282.59 |
```

## How much to set aside

Self-employment tax works out to about 14.1% of net earnings ($282.59 is 14.1% of $2,000). Income tax may come on top once your total income passes your standard deduction. A simple habit is to move a share of every payment into a separate savings account the day you get paid, so the money is there when you file.

If you expect to owe more as your business grows, the IRS expects you to pay during the year in four **estimated tax** payments rather than all at once in April. The IRS explains how on its [estimated taxes page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes), and the rules for self-employment tax are on its [self-employment tax page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes). States may tax business income too.

```takeaways
- Self-employment tax is both halves of Social Security and Medicare: 15.3% on 92.35% of net earnings.
- Net earnings are what you were paid minus business expenses, so keep receipts.
- $400 or more of net self-employment earnings in a year means you must file.
- Set aside part of every payment; self-employment tax alone is about 14.1% of net earnings.
```
