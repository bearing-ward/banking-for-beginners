You may have heard someone say, "I turned down a raise because it would put me in a higher tax bracket." That never makes sense. To see why, you need two ideas: the standard deduction and marginal tax brackets.

## Step 1: the standard deduction

Federal income tax is not charged on every dollar you earn. First you subtract the **standard deduction**, an amount of income that is not taxed at all. In 2026, the standard deduction for a single filer is $16,100. What is left is your **taxable income**.

That is why most teens with a part-time job owe no federal income tax: their earnings for the year are under the standard deduction, so their taxable income is $0. (If a parent claims you as a dependent, your deduction works a little differently; the lesson on whether you need to file explains it.)

Here is Jordan, who started a full-time job after high school at $40,000 a year.

```ledger Jordan's taxable income in 2026
Salary | $40,000.00
- Standard deduction | $16,100.00
= Taxable income | $23,900.00
```

## Step 2: brackets fill up in order

A **tax bracket** is a range of taxable income taxed at one rate. The US uses **marginal rates**: each rate applies only to the dollars inside its range, not to all your income. Think of buckets that fill in order. The first dollars go in the 10% bucket; once it is full, the next dollars go in the 12% bucket, and so on.

```table 2026 federal brackets for a single filer
| Rate | On taxable income |
| 10% | Up to $12,400 |
| 12% | Over $12,400 up to $50,400 |
| 22% | Over $50,400 up to $105,700 |
| 24% | Over $105,700 up to $201,775 |
| 32% | Over $201,775 up to $256,225 |
| 35% | Over $256,225 up to $640,600 |
| 37% | Over $640,600 |
```

Jordan's $23,900 fills the whole 10% bucket ($12,400) and puts the remaining $11,500 in the 12% bucket.

```ledger Jordan's federal income tax
~ Taxable income | $23,900.00
10% of the first $12,400 | $1,240.00
+ 12% of the next $11,500 | $1,380.00
= Federal income tax | $2,620.00
```

Jordan is "in the 12% bracket," but pays far less than 12% of the salary. $2,620 is about 6.6% of $40,000. That overall share is the **effective tax rate**.

## Why a raise never lowers take-home pay

Say Jordan gets a $1,000 raise. Only that new $1,000 is taxed at Jordan's top rate. Every dollar earned before is taxed exactly as it was. Even when a raise pushes some income into a higher bracket, only the dollars over the line pay the higher rate.

```ledger Jordan's $1,000 raise
Raise | $1,000.00
- Extra income tax, 12% of $1,000 | $120.00
- Extra Social Security, 6.2% | $62.00
- Extra Medicare, 1.45% | $14.50
= More take-home pay | $803.50
```

> [!note] These are federal numbers. Most states have their own income tax with their own rates, and a few cities do too. Check your state's department of revenue.

```takeaways
- Taxable income is what you earn minus the standard deduction ($16,100 for a single filer in 2026).
- Each bracket's rate applies only to the dollars inside that bracket.
- Your effective rate, total tax divided by income, is lower than your top bracket.
- A raise always means more take-home pay; only the new dollars are taxed at the higher rate.
```
