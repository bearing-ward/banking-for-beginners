Income tax is figured once a year, but the government does not wait until April to collect it. Instead, your employer takes some out of every paycheck and sends it in for you. That is **withholding**. The form that controls it is the **W-4**, which you fill out when you start a job.

## How withholding works

Your employer does not know your whole year. It looks at one paycheck, assumes you earn about that much every pay period, and uses your W-4 and IRS tables to estimate the tax on a full year. Then it withholds a slice from each check.

When you file your tax return, you compare what was withheld with what you actually owe:

- Withheld more than you owe: you get a **refund** of the difference.
- Withheld less than you owe: you **owe** the difference when you file.

A refund is not a bonus. It is your own money coming back because too much was taken out.

## Why summer jobs often get refunds

Ava works 40 hours a week at $15.00 for 10 weeks in the summer, then stops for the school year. While she works, payroll assumes she earns $600 every week of the year, and about $31,200 a year would owe some income tax. So her employer withholds some. (The paycheck calculator beside this lesson estimates about $30.08 a week; real employer tables vary.)

Her real earnings for 2026 are $6,000. Her parents claim her as a dependent, and her standard deduction covers all of it, so she owes $0. Filing a return gets the withholding back.

```ledger Ava's 2026 return
~ Summer earnings | 10 weeks × $600.00
Federal income tax withheld, about $30.08 a week | $300.80
- Tax actually owed | $0.00
= Refund | $300.80
```

## Why two jobs can mean you owe

Kai works two part-time jobs at the same time and earns $14,000 from each in 2026. Each employer figures withholding as if its job is Kai's only one. On $14,000 alone, Kai would owe no income tax, so neither employer withholds any. But together Kai earned $28,000.

```ledger Kai's two jobs, owed at tax time
Earnings from both jobs | $28,000.00
- Standard deduction | $16,100.00
= Taxable income | $11,900.00
# 10% of $11,900 is $1,190 of federal income tax, and none was withheld.
```

Kai will owe $1,190 when filing. **Step 2** of the W-4 exists for this: if you have more than one job at a time, check its box on each W-4 or use the IRS [Tax Withholding Estimator](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/tax-withholding-estimator) to get the amount right.

## Filling out your W-4

For most teens with one job, the W-4 takes a few minutes:

- **Step 1**: name, address, Social Security number, and filing status (usually "Single").
- **Steps 2 to 4**: only for multiple jobs, children, or other income and deductions. Most teens skip them.
- **Step 5**: sign and date.

```table What the W-4 steps are for
| Step | What it covers | Most teens |
| 1 | Your information and filing status | Fill in |
| 2 | More than one job at a time | Skip unless you have two jobs |
| 3 | Children and other dependents | Skip |
| 4 | Other income, deductions, extra withholding | Skip |
| 5 | Signature | Sign |
```

## Claiming exempt

You can write **Exempt** on your W-4 so no federal income tax is withheld, but only if both are true: you owed no federal income tax last year, and you expect to owe none this year. Exempt stops income tax withholding only; Social Security and Medicare still come out. It also lasts one year at a time, so you need a new W-4 each year to keep it. If you are not sure you qualify, do not claim exempt. Getting a refund is better than owing. See the [IRS page on the W-4](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-4).

```takeaways
- Withholding is income tax paid in advance from each paycheck, based on your W-4.
- A refund means too much was withheld; owing means too little was.
- With two jobs at once, use Step 2 of the W-4 or the IRS estimator.
- Claim exempt only if you owed no federal income tax last year and expect to owe none this year.
```
