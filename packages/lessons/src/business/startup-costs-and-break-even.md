---
title: Startup costs and breaking even
level: next-step
summary: Separate setup costs from monthly and per-sale costs, then work out how many sales would cover them.
---

Priya wants to sell handmade bookmarks at a permitted community market. Before pricing them, she lists what she must buy once, what she pays each month, and what each bookmark costs to make and sell. All prices below are hypothetical.

**Startup costs** are costs to get a business ready to operate. A **fixed cost** stays the same over the activity range you are planning for. A **variable cost** changes with the number of items made or sold. The [SBA's startup-cost guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/calculate-your-startup-costs) explains how to list expenses before launch.

## Sort costs by timing

```table Priya's simplified cost plan
| Cost | Category | Amount |
| Cutting tool and display stand | One-time setup | $45 total |
| One market stall each month | Monthly fixed cost | $25 per month |
| Paper, decoration, sleeve, and selling fee | Variable cost | $2 per bookmark sold |
```

For this example, she makes only what she sells, uses the supplies in that month, and has no other costs. Actual plans may need transport, permits, insurance, waste, taxes, and more. Check local rules with the market and city or county business office. If she buys supplies early, she needs cash before she makes sales.

## What one sale contributes

Priya charges $5 per bookmark. **Contribution margin per unit** is the selling price minus the variable cost of one unit. Here, a unit means one bookmark.

```ledger One hypothetical bookmark sale
Selling price | $5.00
- Variable cost per bookmark | $2.00
= Contribution toward fixed costs and then profit | $3.00
```

That $3 is not all profit yet. It first helps cover the monthly stall fee. If the price is no higher than the variable cost, selling more will not cover positive fixed costs under this model.

## Round up to whole units

**Break-even** is where revenue equals costs, leaving no profit or loss. For one product with unchanged price and costs, divide fixed costs by contribution margin per unit. The [SBA's planning guide](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/plan-your-business/) includes this break-even formula.

Priya's monthly calculation is $25 divided by $3, or about 8.33 bookmarks. She cannot sell one third of a bookmark, so she needs nine sales to cover the listed monthly costs. Eight leave a $1 loss; nine leave $2 before taxes and compensation for her time.

```table Checking whole-bookmark sales for an ordinary month
| | Eight sold | Nine sold |
| Revenue at $5 each | $40 | $45 |
| Variable costs at $2 each | $16 | $18 |
| Monthly fixed cost | $25 | $25 |
| Revenue minus listed costs | $1 loss | $2 left |
```

The ongoing monthly calculation does not recover her $45 setup spending. If she wants to cover that spending in the first month too, she divides $70 by $3. That is about 23.33, so she needs 24 sales.

```ledger First-month cash check at 24 sales
Sales, 24 at $5 | $120.00
- Variable costs, 24 at $2 | $48.00
- Monthly stall fee | $25.00
- One-time setup purchase | $45.00
= Cash left after listed first-month costs | $2.00
```

This is a planning and cash-recovery example, not a tax deduction calculation. A lasting tool can have a different accounting treatment from supplies used up. Her own labor and taxes are excluded from the numbers, so $2 left does not show a sustainable take-home income.

Break-even math tells Priya what sales would be needed. It does not prove that 24 customers will buy. Before purchasing, compare that target with a small demand test and the number she can make in the available time.

```takeaways
- Separate one-time setup, monthly fixed, and per-unit variable costs.
- Price minus variable cost gives the contribution from one sale.
- Divide fixed costs by contribution per unit and round up to whole units.
- State the period and included costs; a sales target does not guarantee demand.
```
