This fictional business sells handmade bookmarks at an approved school market. It combines demand testing, pricing, costs, and a stop-or-continue decision. The exercise excludes taxes and payment fees. A real seller must check those costs, event rules, permits, and age requirements before selling.

## Test before buying a large batch

Tess, 17, has permission to show samples and ask classmates about designs. Ten people say they like them. Only four say they would buy one for $5. Nobody has paid or committed to an order.

Tess has $80 she can afford to risk. A small test needs $30 in fixed costs for display materials and the approved stall. Materials cost $2 per bookmark. Tess can initially make twenty, and production and selling will take six hours in total.

Before reading the answer, calculate the cash needed, the contribution from one sale, and the number of sales needed to cover the $30 fixed cost. Would you buy supplies for a hundred bookmarks immediately?

```ledger Cash needed for the twenty-bookmark test
Display and stall costs | $30.00
+ Materials for twenty at $2 each | $40.00
= Cash needed before selling | $70.00
~ Available risk budget | $80.00
~ Cash remaining before sales | $10.00
```

A positive reaction is useful feedback, not a sale. Tess decides to test a smaller commitment before scaling. The [SBA's market research guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/market-research-competitive-analysis) explains how to investigate demand and customers. A parent or school adviser can help check the event and local requirements.

## Check the break-even estimate

Each $5 sale leaves $3 after its $2 material cost. That **contribution** helps pay the fixed costs. At these assumptions, $30 divided by $3 means ten sales cover the fixed costs and the materials used in those sales. This is an operating break-even estimate, not a guarantee of selling ten.

```ledger If all twenty bookmarks sell
Customer sales, twenty at $5 | $100.00
- Materials for twenty | $40.00
- Fixed test costs | $30.00
= Surplus before tax and paying for Tess's time | $30.00
~ Total time | 6 hours
~ Surplus per hour of Tess's time | $30 ÷ 6 = $5
```

The test could make $30, but that is only $5 for each hour Tess spends, before tax. Business ownership does not promise a good return. Decide whether the learning, return, and risk fit your goals rather than counting every sale as profit.

## Profit estimates do not replace cash planning

If only eight sell, Tess receives $40 after spending $70. She has $50 cash left from her original $80, plus twelve unsold bookmarks. Unsold stock might sell later, but it cannot pay a bill today.

```ledger Cash after eight sales
Original cash | $80.00
- All upfront test spending | $70.00
+ Eight sales at $5 | $40.00
= Cash remaining | $50.00
~ Unsold stock | 12 bookmarks, not counted as cash
```

For a unit-cost view, eight sales bring $40, their materials cost $16, and the fixed test costs are $30, giving a $6 operating loss before tax. The other $24 of materials remains in unsold stock. That is why the $30 cash decrease is not the same as that $6 operating loss. Tax inventory treatment is a separate question.

Before ordering more, Tess should review what sold, ask why others declined, and estimate future demand without counting hoped-for sales as cash. The [SBA break-even guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/calculate-your-startup-costs) explains the assumptions behind the calculation. Continuing, changing the offer, or stopping can each be a reasonable decision.

```takeaways
- Test willingness to pay before buying a large batch.
- Calculate contribution and break-even, but also check cash needed upfront.
- Unsold inventory is not cash, and revenue is not profit.
- Include your time and remaining risk when deciding whether to continue.
```
