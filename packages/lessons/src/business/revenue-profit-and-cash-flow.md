title: Revenue, profit, and cash flow
level: next-step
summary: Sales, money left after costs, and cash available today answer different questions about a small business.

Luis cleans bikes for neighbors. He sees money entering his account and thinks it is all earnings. Some is a customer payment, some is his own savings, and some is borrowed. He needs to label each source.

**Revenue** is money earned from selling goods or services. **Profit** is revenue minus the business expenses for the period. **Cash flow** tracks money actually coming in and going out. All figures below are hypothetical, before taxes.

## Sales are not the amount you keep

Luis completes ten $20 cleanings this month. His only business expenses for that work are $30 of supplies used up and a $10 advertising fee. This example counts revenue when work is completed, even if a customer pays later.

```ledger Luis's completed work this month
Ten bike cleanings at $20 | $200.00
- Supplies used for those cleanings | $30.00
- Advertising | $10.00
= Profit before taxes | $160.00
~ Luis's time | 8 hours
# No employee wages, equipment purchases, or other expenses in this example.
```

Luis has $160 of profit before taxes, not $200. He has not paid himself a wage in this sole-owner example. He should also consider whether that amount is worth his eight hours of work.

## A profitable month can have a cash problem

Only six customers have paid by month-end. The other four owe $80. Luis already paid the $40 of expenses. He starts the month with no business cash.

```ledger Actual cash from this month's operations
Starting business cash | $0.00
+ Six customer payments received | $120.00
- Supplies paid for | $30.00
- Advertising paid for | $10.00
= Cash available at month-end | $80.00
~ Customer payments still owed | $80.00
```

The $160 profit includes completed work that has not all been paid for. The $80 cash balance is what is available now. The [SBA's finance guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/manage-your-finances) explains cash and accrual accounting. **Accrual accounting** records revenue when earned; **cash accounting** generally records it when received. This timing example is not an instruction to choose a tax accounting method.

Luis cannot spend a promise to pay. Before buying more supplies, he checks payment dates and politely follows up on overdue bills using his agreed terms. He can also agree on payment timing before future jobs.

## Funding is not sales

Suppose Luis next puts $50 of his own savings into the business and receives a genuine $100 loan from a relative. Neither payment came from a customer buying a cleaning.

```ledger Cash after adding funding
Cash already available | $80.00
+ Luis's owner contribution | $50.00
+ Loan received | $100.00
= New cash balance | $230.00
~ Loan principal still owed | $100.00
~ New sales from these deposits | $0.00
```

An **owner contribution** is personal money you put into the business. A loan adds cash and a repayment obligation. Neither creates sales revenue or turns an unprofitable business into a profitable one. Repaying loan principal reduces cash but is not a business expense; interest is a separate cost.

The [IRS recordkeeping guide](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p583) recommends identifying deposits as business income, personal funds, or loans. Its [small-business tax guide](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334) explains that genuine loan proceeds are not income. Keep receipts and payment records, and track taxes separately.

Before using the balance in your business account, ask where it came from, what bills are still due, and whether any of it must be repaid.

```takeaways
- Revenue is sales; profit subtracts business expenses.
- Cash flow depends on when money actually arrives and leaves.
- Owner contributions and loans increase cash without increasing sales.
- Label deposits and track unpaid customer bills and loan balances.
```

```quiz
? Luis earns $200 from completed jobs and has $40 of expenses. What is his profit before taxes?
- $200
+ $160
- $40
> Revenue minus expenses is $200 minus $40, or $160. The timing of customer payments can still affect available cash.

? A relative lends Luis $100. How should he label the deposit?
+ Loan funding, with a repayment obligation
- Sales from customers
- Extra profit with no cost
> Borrowing adds cash, but it is not revenue and the principal must be repaid.

? Luis has earned $160 of profit but only $80 of cash. What should he check before spending $120?
- Only the profit total
- Whether his account received any deposit this month
+ When unpaid customers will pay and which bills are due
> Profit does not guarantee cash is available today. Compare actual payment dates with upcoming bills.
```
