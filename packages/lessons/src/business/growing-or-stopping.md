---
title: Growing or stopping a small business
level: next-step
summary: Use real sales and costs to decide whether to grow, adjust, or close a small business. Money already spent is not a reason to keep losing money.
---

When a small business gets orders, it can be tempting to buy more supplies or promise faster service. Before you grow, check whether the business earns enough to cover its costs and whether you can safely handle more work. **Revenue** is money from sales; it is not the same as profit.

## Check what is left after costs

Nora, 17, sells custom cards at a school event. These are fictional figures. After paying for the supplies and table fee, she has $200 left before counting her time, taxes, and any other costs.

```ledger Nora's card sales at one event
Sales | $400.00
- Card stock and envelopes | $160.00
- Event table fee | $40.00
= Cash left before other costs and taxes | $200.00
```

That $200 is not guaranteed take-home pay. Nora still needs to account for any other business costs, applicable taxes, and the value of the hours she worked. The [SBA explains startup and operating costs](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/plan-your-business/), including expenses such as supplies, insurance, and salaries.

## Grow only when the next step makes sense

More orders can also mean more supplies to buy up front, more time, and new permits or safety requirements. Estimate the cost of one next step and compare it with realistic sales. Do not count an order until a customer has actually agreed to it. Do not borrow money based on a promise that sales will keep rising.

```table A decision before making more cards
| Question | Nora's check |
| Are customers asking for more? | Ask customers what they want before buying supplies |
| Can she cover the next supply order? | Check cash on hand after bills and taxes set aside |
| Can she fill orders on time? | Compare expected hours with school and other commitments |
| Are there new rules or risks? | Check event, tax, permit, and safety requirements |
```

## Adjust or stop when the numbers or workload do not work

After a few events, Nora's costs remain higher than the money she brings in. She has already spent $60 on a custom sign, but the sign cannot be returned. That $60 is a **sunk cost**: money already spent that cannot be recovered. It matters in her records, but it should not force her to keep losing more money. She can change the offer, pause sales, or stop and settle any orders or bills she already accepted.

```table Options when a small business is not working
| Option | Before choosing, check |
| Adjust | Whether a smaller menu, different price, or lower costs is realistic |
| Pause | Whether any open orders, deposits, or bills still need attention |
| Stop | How to tell customers, settle what you owe, and close permits or registrations |
```

If you registered an LLC or corporation, stopping sales may not end the legal entity's filings or taxes. Follow the state's closure process and check the [SBA guidance for managing and closing a business](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/manage-your-business/). For a teen, ask a parent or guardian and a local small business counselor to help finish paperwork and handle customer commitments.

No business can promise a certain income. Review sales and costs after each event or month, then choose a next step you can afford. If stopping is the best choice, that is a business decision, not proof you failed.

```takeaways
- Revenue is not profit; subtract business costs and account for taxes and time.
- Estimate the cost, demand, workload, and safety needs before growing.
- Money already spent cannot be recovered; do not keep losing money only to justify it.
- If you stop, settle open orders and follow the closure rules for any registered entity.
```
