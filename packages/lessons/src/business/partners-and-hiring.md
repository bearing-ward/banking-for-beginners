A friend may offer to help sell bracelets or run a weekend lawn-care business with you. Before you share ownership or pay someone, be clear about who owns what, who does which work, and what rules apply. If you are under 18, ask a parent or guardian to help check what you can sign and which adult support is needed in your state.

## Put a partnership in writing

A **partner** is a co-owner of a business. A friendly handshake does not answer what happens when someone contributes more time, wants to leave, or disagrees about spending. Depending on state law, two people who co-own and operate a business for profit may be treated as a partnership even if they never filed paperwork. The [SBA's guide to business structures](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/launch-your-business/) explains that a partnership has different ownership and responsibility rules from a sole proprietorship.

Before working together, write down the ownership shares, each person's duties, how decisions are made, how costs and profits are split, and what happens if a partner leaves or the business stops. Check state requirements for a partnership agreement or other formation documents. Have an adult, lawyer, or small business counselor review important terms.

```table Topics for a written partner agreement
| Topic | Question to answer |
| Ownership | What share does each person own, and what did each contribute? |
| Work | Who handles orders, supplies, customer messages, and records? |
| Money | How are business costs, losses, and profits shared? |
| Decisions | Who can approve purchases or sign agreements? |
| Exit or disagreement | How can someone leave, and how will a dispute be handled? |
```

## Do not label a worker to avoid responsibilities

If you pay someone to do work, figure out whether they are an **employee** or an **independent contractor** under the rules that apply. The label in a text, agreement, or tax form does not decide by itself. The actual work relationship matters, and federal tax, wage, and state rules can differ. The [Department of Labor explains worker classification](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/flsa/misclassification).

An employee can mean payroll records, tax withholding and reporting, wage rules, and workplace safety responsibilities. A contractor is running an independent business, not simply a friend you call an independent contractor. Before hiring, ask what federal and state obligations apply. The [IRS guide for small businesses](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/employment-taxes) covers employment taxes.

```table Before paying someone to work
| Question | Why check |
| Who controls when and how the work is done? | The real work arrangement can matter for classification |
| Is the worker in business for themself? | A title alone does not make someone a contractor |
| What payroll and tax steps apply? | Employers may have withholding, reporting, and deposit duties |
| What safety and insurance rules apply? | State and federal requirements depend on the job and location |
```

If a worker is under 18, extra rules may limit work hours, tasks, or equipment. Federal and state child labor laws both may apply; follow the stricter rule where they differ. Check the [DOL youth employment guidance](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/child-labor) and your state labor office before scheduling a teen. Do not ask a friend to use dangerous equipment or work hours that may violate the rules.

If you are a teen considering a partner or helper, pause before taking money or promising ownership. Ask an adult to help you check the agreement and the job rules, then make sure everyone has a copy.

```takeaways
- Agree in writing on ownership, jobs, money, decisions, leaving, and disputes.
- A worker's actual arrangement matters; calling a friend a contractor does not settle classification.
- Employees can create payroll, tax, safety, and insurance duties.
- Check both federal and state rules before hiring or scheduling a minor.
```
