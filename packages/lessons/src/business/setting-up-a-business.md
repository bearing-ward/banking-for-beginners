Maybe you bake for neighbors, make art, or walk dogs for pay. **Business structure** means the legal setup for who owns the business and who is responsible for its debts and decisions. The right setup depends on your activity, location, and risks. You do not have to form an LLC just because you have a business idea.

## Know the basic choices

A **sole proprietorship** is one owner doing business without forming a separate legal entity. It can be simpler to start, but the owner and business are not legally separate, so business debts or claims can reach the owner's personal assets. A **limited liability company (LLC)** is a state-created entity that can separate some business and personal responsibilities. That protection has limits: it is not a shield for every action, debt, or personal guarantee, and an LLC can involve filing fees and ongoing state requirements. A **corporation** is a separate legal entity with more formal rules and different tax choices.

```table A first look at business structures
| Structure | Basic idea | What to check |
| Sole proprietorship | One owner; no separate business entity | Personal responsibility for business debts and claims |
| LLC | State-created entity with some separation of owner and business | State fees, reports, taxes, insurance, and limits of protection |
| Corporation | Entity separate from its owners, with formal records and rules | Formation costs, required records, taxes, and ongoing filings |
```

The [Small Business Administration's structure guide](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/launch-your-business/) explains these trade-offs. It is general information; rules and costs vary by state. Compare costs and risks before filing. An LLC is not automatically the best choice for a small teen business.

## Check requirements before spending

Before taking orders, check your state, county, and city rules for business-name registration, licenses, permits, sales taxes, and **zoning** rules about what activities are allowed where you live. Requirements depend on the work and location. Food sales, for example, may have health rules. Ask a parent or guardian and a local small business counselor to help you find official requirements.

An **employer identification number (EIN)** is a federal tax ID for a business. The IRS requires one for some situations, including having employees or certain business tax filings. A sole proprietor may not need one for federal tax purposes, but a bank or state may ask for one. Check the [IRS EIN page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/employer-identification-number) before applying; the IRS issues EINs for free.

```table Before you accept your first order
| Check | Where to ask | Why it matters |
| Age and signatures | Parent or guardian; state and local offices | Some contracts, accounts, or registrations may have age rules |
| Business name and registration | State business office | A trade name or business entity may need registration |
| Permits, zoning, and sales tax | City, county, and state government | Rules depend on the activity and location |
| EIN and federal tax steps | IRS.gov | You may need an EIN or tax filings based on your setup |
| Insurance and liability | State resources or an insurance professional | A structure does not cover every risk |
```

If you are under 18, involve a parent or guardian before signing forms, opening accounts, accepting platform terms, or agreeing to pay for a lease or service. The legal ability to form or run a business can depend on your state and the contract. An adult can help check the requirements without guessing.

## Separate business money and records

Track every sale and every business cost. Keep receipts and a simple list of dates, amounts, and what each transaction was for. The [IRS explains why businesses should keep clear income and expense records](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/recordkeeping). Ask a bank what documents and adult involvement it requires for an account. Separate business money from personal money when you can; it makes the records easier to understand.

An **owner's draw** is money an owner moves from the business to personal use. For a sole proprietor, taking money out is not itself a business expense for tax purposes. Keep it in your cash records, but list actual business costs separately.

```table Sample money record for a card-making business
| Transaction | Business cash change | Business cost for this example? |
| Customer pays for cards | +$80.00 | No, this is sales income |
| Card stock and envelopes | -$25.00 | Yes, supplies used for the business |
| Owner moves money to personal savings | -$20.00 | No, this is an owner's draw |
| Business cash left from these transactions | $35.00 | Cash balance, not the same as profit for tax purposes |
```

Start with a small plan: list the rules to check, estimate startup costs, record each transaction, and ask for adult help with anything you must sign.

```takeaways
- A sole proprietorship, LLC, and corporation have different costs, responsibilities, and legal effects.
- Check age rules, local permits, zoning, taxes, and insurance needs before spending.
- An EIN is required in some situations; check IRS rules before applying.
- Keep business transactions separate from personal spending, and record owner draws correctly.
```
