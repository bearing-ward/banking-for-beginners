After high school, a lot of people assume the only choice is four years at a university. It is one good path. But there are others, and some of them let you earn while you learn or finish with little or no debt.

```table Paths after high school
| Path | How long | How it is paid for |
| Community college | 2 years, or transfer after | Low tuition; grants through the FAFSA |
| Trade or technical school | Months to 2 years | Tuition; grants and loans at many schools |
| Apprenticeship | 1 to 5 years | You are paid while you train |
| Military service | Varies | Pay, training, and education benefits |
| Four-year college | 4 years or more | Grants, scholarships, savings, loans |
```

## Community college, then transfer

**Community colleges** are public two-year schools that usually cost much less than a university. You can earn a certificate or an associate degree, or take your first two years of classes and then transfer to a university to finish a bachelor's degree. This is often called a **2 + 2** plan.

Say community college tuition and fees are $4,000 a year and the state university's are $11,000 a year. (Prices vary a lot by state and school, so look up real ones.)

```ledger The 2 + 2 plan
~ Community college | 2 years × $4,000
~ University | 2 years × $11,000
Two years at community college | $8,000.00
+ Two years at the university | $22,000.00
= Tuition and fees for a bachelor's degree | $30,000.00
```

```ledger Compared with four years at the university
~ University | 4 years × $11,000
Four years at the university | $44,000.00
- The 2 + 2 plan | $30,000.00
= Saved | $14,000.00
```

Before you start, check that your credits will transfer. Many states have transfer agreements between community colleges and state universities; ask a counselor at both schools.

## Trade school and apprenticeships

Electricians, plumbers, welders, HVAC technicians, dental assistants, and medical coders often train at a **trade school** or through an **apprenticeship**. In a **registered apprenticeship**, an employer pays you while you learn on the job and take classes, and your pay usually rises as your skills grow. Search programs at [Apprenticeship.gov](https://www.apprenticeship.gov), run by the U.S. Department of Labor.

Before you sign up for a trade school, check that it is accredited, and ask how many students finish and how many find jobs in the field. Be wary of high-pressure sales and promises of guaranteed jobs. The [FTC has tips on choosing a vocational school](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/choosing-vocational-school-or-certificate-program).

## Work-study and the military

**Federal Work-Study** gives students with financial need a part-time job, often on campus, to help pay for school. You get considered for it by filing the FAFSA and checking that you are interested.

The military offers pay, job training, and education benefits such as the GI Bill, and ROTC programs can pay for college in exchange for service after you graduate. It is a serious commitment of several years, so talk with family and read the details at [VA.gov](https://www.va.gov/education/) before you meet with a recruiter.

```takeaways
- Community college and a transfer can cut the cost of a bachelor's degree.
- Apprenticeships pay you while you train for skilled work.
- Check accreditation and job results before choosing a trade school.
- File the FAFSA no matter which path you take; it covers work-study and many programs.
```
