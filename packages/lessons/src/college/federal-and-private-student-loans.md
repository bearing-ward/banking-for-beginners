After grants, scholarships, savings, and work, many students still have a gap. A **student loan** fills it. You borrow now and repay after school, with interest. There are two main kinds, and the difference matters for years.

**Federal student loans** come from the U.S. Department of Education. You get them by filing the FAFSA, and most do not need a credit history. **Private student loans** come from banks, credit unions, and other lenders. They work more like a car loan: the lender checks credit, and a teen usually needs a **cosigner**, often a parent, who is equally responsible for every payment.

## Federal versus private

```table How the two kinds compare
| | Federal loans | Private loans |
| Credit check | Not for most student loans | Yes; usually needs a cosigner |
| Interest rate | Fixed, set by law each year | Fixed or variable, based on credit |
| Payments based on income | Yes, through income-driven repayment | Rarely |
| Pausing payments | Options in some situations, such as going back to school | Up to the lender |
| Forgiveness programs | Some, for certain jobs and situations | Rarely |
```

**Income-driven repayment** sets your monthly payment based on how much you earn, which can keep payments manageable if your first job pays less than you hoped. Federal repayment rules change from time to time, so check the current options at [StudentAid.gov](https://studentaid.gov/manage-loans/repayment).

## Subsidized and unsubsidized

There are two main kinds of federal loans for undergraduates.

- A **subsidized loan** is for students with financial need. The government pays the interest while you are in school at least half time.
- An **unsubsidized loan** is available whether or not you have need. Interest starts adding up from the day the money is paid out, even while you are in class.

Here is what that means for $5,000 borrowed freshman year. Say the rate is 6% (it is only an example; the real rate is set each year), and you make no payments during four years of school.

```ledger $5,000 unsubsidized loan, at an example 6%
~ Interest each year | 6% of $5,000
Amount borrowed | $5,000.00
+ Interest, year 1 | $300.00
+ Interest, year 2 | $300.00
+ Interest, year 3 | $300.00
+ Interest, year 4 | $300.00
= Owed at graduation | $6,200.00
```

With a subsidized loan for the same amount, you would owe $5,000 at graduation. You can pay the interest on an unsubsidized loan while you are in school, even $25 a month, to keep the balance from growing.

## Borrow in the right order

1. Grants, scholarships, savings, and earnings first.
2. Federal subsidized loans.
3. Federal unsubsidized loans.
4. Private loans only after federal options are used up, and only after comparing rates.

You do not have to accept every loan in your aid offer, and you can accept less than the amount offered. The [CFPB's guide to paying for college](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/paying-for-college/) has more help comparing offers.

```takeaways
- Federal loans come through the FAFSA and offer protections such as income-driven repayment.
- Private loans need good credit or a cosigner and usually have fewer protections.
- Subsidized loans do not grow while you are in school; unsubsidized loans do.
- Use free money first, then federal loans, and private loans last.
```
