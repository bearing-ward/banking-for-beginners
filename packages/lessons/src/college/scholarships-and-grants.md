The best money for college is money you never repay. That is what **grants** and **scholarships** are. Use every bit of them you can get before you borrow a dollar.

## Two kinds of free money

A **grant** is usually based on **financial need**: how much your family can afford to pay. Grants come from the federal government (such as the Federal Pell Grant), your state, and colleges themselves. You apply for most of them by filing the FAFSA.

A **scholarship** is usually based on something you did or are: grades, a sport, art or music, a career goal, community service, where you live, or where a parent works. Scholarships come from colleges, local groups, businesses, and nonprofits.

```table Where money for college comes from
| Type | Based on | Pay it back? |
| Grant | Mostly financial need | No |
| Scholarship | Merit, talent, or background | No |
| Work-study | Need; you earn it at a job | No, you work for it |
| Student loan | Enrollment | Yes, with interest |
```

A grant can have conditions. If you drop out partway through a term, you may have to return some of it, so ask the financial aid office before you withdraw.

## Small scholarships add up

Big national scholarships get thousands of applications. Local ones, from your town's clubs, a credit union, a parent's employer, or a community foundation, get far fewer. Your odds are better, and you can win several.

```ledger Elena's local scholarships, senior year
Rotary club scholarship | $500.00
+ Credit union essay contest | $1,000.00
+ Mom's employer scholarship | $750.00
+ Community foundation award | $250.00
= Free money for her first year | $2,500.00
```

That $2,500 is $2,500 Elena does not have to borrow or pay interest on.

## Where to look

- Your school counselor's list of local scholarships.
- The financial aid page of each college you apply to. Many give automatic merit scholarships.
- Your state's higher education agency, for state grants.
- The U.S. Department of Labor's free [scholarship finder](https://www.careeronestop.org/toolkit/training/find-scholarships.aspx).
- Groups you belong to: places of worship, clubs, unions, and employers.

Keep a list of deadlines and reuse your best essays. Tell your college about any outside scholarship you win; it can change the rest of your aid offer, so ask how.

## Scholarship scams

You should never pay to apply for or receive a scholarship. Scammers charge "processing fees," sell "guaranteed" scholarships, or ask for your bank account to "deposit" an award.

```message A scholarship you never applied for
Channel: text
From: +1 (555) 014-8820
Congratulations! You have been selected for the National Achievers $5,000 Scholarship. Pay a $49 processing fee in the next 48 hours to claim your award.
! An award you never applied for
! A fee to receive free money
! A short deadline to rush you
```

If an offer asks you to pay, walk away. The [FTC explains scholarship and financial aid scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-scholarship-and-financial-aid-scams), and you can report one at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov).

```takeaways
- Grants and scholarships do not have to be paid back; use them before loans.
- File the FAFSA to be considered for most grants.
- Local scholarships are smaller but easier to win, and they add up.
- Never pay to apply for or receive a scholarship.
```
