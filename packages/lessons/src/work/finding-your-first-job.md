Sam is 16 and wants money for a car fund. Sam has never had a job, has no resume, and is not sure who would hire someone with no experience. Plenty of places would. Most first jobs are built for people exactly like Sam.

A first job is also where you learn the things this whole topic covers: forms, pay stubs, taxes, and how to read the money that lands in your account.

## Where to look

Start close to home. Stores, restaurants, grocery stores, movie theaters, pools, camps, and libraries hire teens every season, often before summer. Many post openings on their own websites; others put a sign in the window. Your school counselor may know local employers, and many cities run **summer youth employment programs** that match teens with paid work.

You can also make your own job: babysitting, pet sitting, tutoring, or mowing lawns. That income counts too, and it is taxed a little differently (see the lesson on gig work).

```table Where first jobs come from
| Where | Examples | How to apply |
| Stores and restaurants | Grocery clerk, cashier, host, barista | Online application or in person |
| Seasonal work | Lifeguard, camp counselor, theme park | Apply early, often in late winter or spring |
| City or school programs | Summer youth jobs, library aide | School counselor or city website |
| Your own work | Babysitting, pet sitting, lawn care | Word of mouth, neighbors, family friends |
```

## Applying with no experience

An **application** asks for your contact information, your availability, and sometimes past work. If you have never had a job, list what you have done: school clubs, sports, volunteering, chores you were trusted with, or babysitting for neighbors. Employers hiring teens mostly want to know that you show up on time, follow directions, and are kind to customers.

Be honest about your **availability**, the hours you can work. Saying yes to shifts you cannot cover is the fastest way to lose a first job.

## References

A **reference** is someone who can tell an employer what you are like. Good choices are a teacher, coach, club advisor, or a family you have babysat for. Family members usually do not count. Always ask first, and give them a heads-up about the job so they are ready for the call.

```message Asking a coach to be a reference
Channel: email
From: Sam Rivera
Subject: Could you be a reference for me?
Hi Coach Patel, I am applying for a cashier job at a grocery store near school. Would you be willing to be a reference? They may call you this month. Thank you for thinking about it!
```

## Work permits

Some states require workers under 18 to get a **work permit**, sometimes called working papers, before starting a job. You usually get one through your school or your state's labor department, and a parent may need to sign it. Rules differ by state, so look yours up on the [Department of Labor's state child labor page](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/state/child-labor) or ask your school counselor.

## Know what a job is worth

Before you say yes, do the math. Multiply the hourly wage by the hours you expect to work. That is your **gross pay**, before taxes come out. The lesson on pay stubs shows what is left after.

```ledger Sam's summer job, before taxes
~ Hourly wage | $14.00
~ Hours a week | 15
~ Weeks | 10
Gross pay for the summer (15 × $14.00 × 10) | $2,100.00
= Total before taxes | $2,100.00
```

> [!warning] A real job never asks you to pay to be hired, buy your own gift cards, or deposit a check and send part of it back. Those are scams. You can report one at [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov).

```takeaways
- Most first jobs are built for people with no experience; list school, sports, volunteering, and babysitting instead.
- Ask references before you list them, and pick people who are not family.
- Some states require a work permit for workers under 18; check with your school or state.
- A job that asks you to pay money up front is a scam.
```
