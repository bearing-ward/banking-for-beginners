Maya finished her first two weeks as a lifeguard. She worked 20 hours at $15.00 an hour, so she expected $300. Her deposit was $277.05. Nothing went wrong: the difference is on her **pay stub**, the record that comes with every paycheck.

A pay stub has three parts: what you earned, what was taken out, and what you actually got.

```ledger Maya's pay stub, one pay period
~ Hours worked | 20
~ Hourly rate | $15.00 an hour
Gross pay | $300.00
- Social Security (6.2%) | $18.60
- Medicare (1.45%) | $4.35
- Federal income tax | $0.00
- State income tax | $0.00
= Net pay | $277.05
```

## Gross pay and net pay

**Gross pay** is everything you earned before anything comes out: hours times your rate, plus any overtime or tips your employer reports. **Net pay** is what is left after taxes and other deductions. It is the number that lands in your bank account, so it is the number to plan with.

> [!tip] When someone asks what a job pays, they usually mean gross pay. When you build a budget, use net pay.

## FICA: Social Security and Medicare

Almost every paycheck has two lines that together are called **FICA**. Social Security takes 6.2% of your gross pay and Medicare takes 1.45%. Your employer pays the same amount again on top. This money funds retirement and health care for older Americans, and working now is how you earn your own future benefits.

Unlike income tax, FICA comes out of the very first dollar you earn. That is why Maya paid it even on a small check.

```ledger Where FICA comes from
~ Gross pay | $300.00
Social Security, 6.2% of $300 | $18.60
+ Medicare, 1.45% of $300 | $4.35
= Total FICA | $22.95
```

## Income tax withholding

Your employer also sets aside **federal income tax** based on the W-4 form you filled out when you were hired. In 2026, a single person owes no federal income tax on the first $16,100 they earn in the year, which is the **standard deduction**. Maya's summer job will not reach that, so her employer withheld nothing.

Some states have their own income tax and some do not. If yours does, you will see a state line too, and sometimes a local one.

## Year to date

Most stubs also show a **year-to-date** (YTD) column: the running total since January 1. Check it once in a while. It is how you know how much you earned this year, and it should match the W-2 form your employer sends in January.

```table This period and year to date
| | This period | Year to date |
| Gross pay | $300.00 | $1,500.00 |
| Social Security | $18.60 | $93.00 |
| Medicare | $4.35 | $21.75 |
| Net pay | $277.05 | $1,385.25 |
```

## Check every stub

Payroll mistakes happen. Each time you get paid, check that your hours and rate are right, and that your name and the last four digits of your Social Security number are correct. If something looks wrong, ask your manager or payroll right away. Keep your stubs, or screenshots of them, until you have filed that year's taxes.

```takeaways
- Gross pay is what you earned; net pay is what you get. Budget with net pay.
- Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) come out of every paycheck.
- Federal income tax withholding depends on your W-4; small earners often owe none.
- Check your hours, rate, and year-to-date totals on every stub.
```
