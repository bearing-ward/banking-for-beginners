Diego is 15 and recently started bagging groceries. His friend Ana, 17, works the register at the same store. They get different schedules and different tasks, and that is not the manager playing favorites. Federal and state law set rules for teen workers, and the rules change with age.

## Minimum wage

The **minimum wage** is the lowest hourly pay an employer is allowed to pay. In 2026, the federal minimum wage is $7.25 an hour. Many states, and some cities, set a higher minimum. When more than one applies, your employer must pay the highest one.

There are a few exceptions. Workers who earn tips can be paid a lower cash wage, as long as tips bring them up to at least the minimum. Federal law also allows a lower "youth minimum wage" for a short time when a young worker first starts, though some states do not. Look up your state's numbers on the [Department of Labor's state minimum wage page](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/minimum-wage/state).


## When you can work at 14 and 15

The federal law that covers teen work is the **Fair Labor Standards Act** (FLSA). For 14 and 15 year olds, it limits:

- How many hours you can work on a school day and in a school week
- How many hours you can work on a day or week without school
- How early you can start and how late you can work, with later hours allowed in summer

It also limits the kind of work. At 14 and 15 you can usually work in retail, food service, offices, and similar places, but not with most power-driven machines, and only some kinds of cooking.

It is OK to say no. Try: "I'm 15, so I'm not allowed to work that late. I can come in early this weekend instead."

## At 16 and 17

Federal law does not limit hours for 16 and 17 year olds, but no one under 18 can do jobs the Department of Labor calls **hazardous**. Examples include driving as part of the job in most cases, running a forklift, using a power-driven meat slicer or bakery mixer, and roofing.

```table Federal teen work rules at a glance
| Age | Hours | Jobs |
| 14 and 15 | Limited hours and times, tighter on school days | Retail, food service, office; no hazardous work and limits on machines and cooking |
| 16 and 17 | No federal hour limits | Most jobs, but no hazardous work |
| 18 and up | No limits for teens | Any job |
```

## Your state may be stricter

States can set their own rules for teen workers, and many do: more hour limits, required breaks, or a work permit. When federal and state rules differ, your employer must follow the one that protects you more. The Department of Labor lists [the federal youth rules](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/child-labor) and [state child labor laws](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/state/child-labor).

> [!tip] If you think you are being paid less than the minimum or scheduled illegally, write down your hours and pay, and talk to a parent or school counselor. You can also contact the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division for free.

```takeaways
- In 2026 the federal minimum wage is $7.25 an hour; your state or city may require more, and the highest one wins.
- At 14 and 15, federal law limits your hours and how late you can work.
- No one under 18 can do hazardous jobs like running a forklift or a meat slicer.
- Look up your state's rules; they can be stricter than federal law.
```
