Driving is the biggest financial risk most teens take on. One crash can cause tens of thousands of dollars in damage and medical bills. Car insurance pays for much of that, and in nearly every state, driving without it is against the law.

## The main parts of a policy

**Liability coverage** pays for injuries and damage you cause to other people when a crash is your fault. It does not pay for your own car. Nearly every state requires a minimum amount. Your state sets the minimums, and many drivers choose more, because a serious crash can cost far more than the minimum covers.

```table What each coverage pays for
| Coverage | Pays for | Required? |
| Liability | Other people's injuries and property, when you are at fault | In nearly every state |
| Collision | Your car, after a crash, minus your deductible | Optional, but lenders usually require it |
| Comprehensive | Your car, after theft, hail, fire, or hitting a deer, minus your deductible | Optional, but lenders usually require it |
| Uninsured motorist | You, when the at-fault driver has no or too little insurance | Required in some states |
| Medical payments or PIP | Medical bills for you and your passengers | Required in some states |
```

## How a claim works

Mateo, 18, backs into a pole. The repair estimate for his car is $3,200. He has collision coverage with a $500 deductible.

```ledger Mateo's collision claim
Repair estimate | $3,200.00
- Mateo pays his deductible | $500.00
= Insurance pays the shop | $2,700.00
```

If Mateo had only liability coverage, he would pay the full $3,200 himself, because liability covers damage to others, not to your own car. For an older, low-value car, some drivers drop collision and comprehensive to save money, knowing they would cover repairs themselves.

## Why teens pay more

Insurers set prices based on how likely a group is to crash. New drivers, especially teens, have the highest crash rates of any age group, so their premiums are the highest. Prices fall as you build a record of safe driving, usually year by year into your mid-20s. Where you live, the car you drive, and your driving record also matter.

## Ways to lower the price

- Stay on a parent's policy instead of buying your own, which is usually cheaper.
- Ask about a **good student discount**, often for a B average or better.
- Finish an approved driver education or defensive driving course.
- Choose a safer, less expensive car to insure.
- Pick a higher deductible, if you have the savings to cover it.
- Keep your record clean: tickets and at-fault crashes raise premiums for years.

Here is an illustrative example. Real quotes and discount amounts vary by insurer and state.

```ledger The cost of adding Riley to a family policy, per year
Added premium for Riley | $2,400.00
- Good student discount (10%) | $240.00
- Driver education discount (5%) | $120.00
= Riley's added cost | $2,040.00
~ Per month | $170
```

Get quotes from at least three insurers, with the same coverage and deductibles, so you are comparing like with like. Your state's insurance department, which you can find through the [NAIC](https://www.naic.org), explains the minimums where you live and handles complaints.

```takeaways
- Liability insurance pays for damage you cause to others and is required in nearly every state.
- Collision and comprehensive cover your own car, minus a deductible.
- Teen premiums are high because new drivers crash more; good grades, courses, and a clean record help.
- Compare quotes with identical coverage before you buy.
```
